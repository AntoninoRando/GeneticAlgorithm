The code

Data pipeline

How one simulator log becomes 30 training worlds, and how a CSV row becomes a Job or a Satellite. The full technical write-up is data_processing/DATA_PIPELINE.md.

Where the data comes from

data/example.csv is the task log of one 300-minute run of the discrete-event simulator of Magliarisi et al. (magliarisi2025orbit). That simulator uses real Starlink orbits and models each satellite as a queue. The log has one row per task: its type, execution and transfer time, image size, number of routing hops, queue length, the server (satellite) that ran it, and its remaining energy. config.json5 holds the simulator settings that produced it.

The file is not in git. Ask your supervisor for it.

Step 1: split into worlds

world_splitter.py puts each row into a 10-minute window, \([0,10), [10,20), \dots\), using its Arrival Time (System). Each non-empty window becomes a world: 30 files of about 100 tasks each, plus a manifest.json.

Inside each world the arrival times are shifted so the earliest is 0, keeping the gaps, and written to a new column Arrival Time (Normalized). The absolute minute does not matter to the scheduler; the order and spacing of arrivals do.

Why worlds at all?

Training on one fixed instance invites overfitting: the formula learns the quirks of that instance. The papers always train on several instances (dtgpam §V-A samples 5 per generation; zhang2025mtgper averages 100 simulations). Here the population switches to the next world when it stops improving (main.py), or is scored on several worlds at once with --worlds-per-eval. Yang calls cycling through instances "rotation" (yang2025thesis §3.6).

Step 2: build jobs (create_jobs_from_csv)

For each of the first --csv-row-limit rows (default 100) of a world:

  • Rescaling. Raw values are mapped linearly into fixed ranges using the world's min and max: executionTime → 5–30 min (rounded), transferTime → 1–8 min, imageSize → 8–24. The formula is \(\text{dst}_{\min} + \frac{x - \text{src}_{\min}}{\text{src}_{\max} - \text{src}_{\min}}(\text{dst}_{\max}-\text{dst}_{\min})\), clipped to the range (utils._rescale).
  • RAM and disk depend on the task type and the rescaled queue length \(q \in [0,1]\): CPU-and-data-intensive jobs get \(8 + 8q\) RAM and \(3\times\) image size of disk, CPU-intensive jobs \(4 + 8q\) and \(2\times\), all others \(1 + 5q\) and \(1.5\times\).
  • Hops are clipped to 1–5. Arrival is the normalised arrival rounded to a whole minute (Job.arrivalTime is an int).
  • Missing values are replaced by the midpoint of the observed range.
Rescaling is per world

Because min and max are taken per world, a job with executionTime = 30 in world 3 is not necessarily as long, in raw terms, as one with 30 in world 7. The formula only sees values relative to their world.

Step 3: build satellites (create_satellites_from_csv)

The CSV has no satellite table, so satellites are inferred from the servers that appear in the world's rows. For each distinct Server Name the code averages execution time, transfer time, queue length and remaining energy over that server's rows, then rescales them:

A small random perturbation (±2%) keeps satellites distinct even when their statistics coincide. The random draws use a per-world seed taken from the run seed, so --seed makes the worlds reproducible too.

Energy is the binding constraint

With an energy rate of 1 per minute and job durations of 6–38 minutes, a budget of 14–26 units fits only one or two jobs per satellite. The sunset (300+ minutes away) almost never binds. This is the main reason completion ratios are only about 0.2–0.27 in every experiment. Keep it in mind before reading anything into a low \(C\). See Known quirks.

Step 4: the snapshot

main.py's build_world() sets the snapshot time \(\tau\) to the latest arrival in the world, so every job has arrived. It then creates SimulationSnapshot(τ, satellites, jobs) with the default uniform energy rate 1.0. Every satellite becomes free at \(\tau\). Response time is still measured from each job's own arrival, so jobs that arrived early start with a handicap of up to 10 minutes.

Gotchas

  • Header names are matched after stripping whitespace: the raw header is " Arrival Time (System) ".
  • Numbers use European formatting in places (12.345.678, 2,17E+10). utils._parse_csv_number handles it, so do not use float() directly.
  • Columns listed in _IGNORED_CSV_COLUMNS (task ID, status, start/end time, server name…) are deliberately not used as job features. Most of them are outcomes of the simulator's own scheduler and would leak the answer.
