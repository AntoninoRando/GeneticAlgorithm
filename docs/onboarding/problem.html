Concepts

The scheduling problem

What we are trying to optimise, first in plain words, then as maths. The formal version is in thesis/chapters/problem.tex.

The real-world setting

Satellite edge computing (SEC) puts small computers on satellites in low Earth orbit (LEO), a few hundred kilometres up. Instead of sending raw data down to a data centre, the satellites process it themselves (leyva2023sec, wang2023satellite).

A ground user sends a task (we call it a job in the code) to an access point, a satellite currently overhead. The access point decides which satellite in its neighbourhood runs the job. In the research our data comes from, each of these satellites is called a satellite edge node (SEN) (magliarisi2025orbit).

Two things make this hard:

  • Satellites move. A LEO satellite is visible from a point on the ground for only a few minutes. After its sunset it can no longer return results to that user. A job must finish before then.
  • Satellites run on batteries. Each one has a limited energy budget. A job that needs more energy than is left cannot run.

The scheduler also has to be cheap. A satellite cannot afford to solve a big optimisation problem for every request. Magliarisi and Casalicchio chose fast heuristics over integer linear programming (ILP), genetic algorithms or deep learning for exactly this reason (icdcs_sc). Evolving a formula respects that constraint: the expensive search happens offline, on the ground, and the satellite only evaluates the evolved formula.

A snapshot

We do not simulate time passing. We take a snapshot: a frozen picture of the world at one instant, containing all satellites and all jobs (about 100) that arrived in a 10-minute window. The snapshot time is set to the last arrival, so every job is waiting. Then we ask: "if you had to place all these waiting jobs now, how would you do it?" In the code this is SimulationSnapshot in fitness_evaluator.h.

This is a batch, static version of the problem. The papers we build on mostly study the dynamic version, where decisions are made one at a time as jobs arrive (nguyen2017gpsurvey). See open questions.

The maths

Notation (it matches the thesis):

  • Jobs \(j \in J\), with arrival time \(a_j\), execution time \(e_j\), transfer time \(t_j\), and duration \(d_j = e_j + t_j\).
  • Satellites \(s \in S\), with sunset \(\omega_s\), remaining energy \(E_s\) and energy rate \(\rho_s\) (energy per minute, 1 in all our runs).
  • \(f_s\) is the minute at which satellite \(s\) becomes free. It starts at the snapshot time \(\tau\) and grows as jobs are added.

Constraints

Job \(j\) may be placed on satellite \(s\), starting at \(f_s\), only if both hold:

$$ \underbrace{f_s + d_j \le \omega_s}_{\text{finish before sunset}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \underbrace{\rho_s\, d_j \le E_s}_{\text{enough energy}} $$

Each satellite runs one job at a time, without interruption (non-preemptive, single machine per satellite). When a job is committed, \(f_s \leftarrow f_s + d_j\) and \(E_s \leftarrow E_s - \rho_s d_j\).

Why these constraints

The sunset constraint is the "orbit-aware" rule of Magliarisi et al.: the response time must be shorter than the satellite's remaining visibility (magliarisi2025orbit §5.3, magliarisi2026thesis §6.3.1). The energy test is the battery-budget check of icdcs_sc §III-C. Both are hard: an infeasible pair is never considered, whatever score the formula gives it. The per-job deadline constraint of those papers is missing here, because our jobs have no deadline field.

Objectives

A schedule \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of committed (job, satellite) pairs, with completion time \(c_j\) for each scheduled job. Over \(N = |J|\) jobs:

$$ \begin{aligned} C &= \frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{N} && \text{completion ratio (maximise)}\\[4pt] R &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{(j,s)\in\mathcal{A}} (c_j - a_j) && \text{mean response time over scheduled jobs (minimise)}\\[4pt] \mathcal{E} &= \sum_{(j,s)\in\mathcal{A}} \rho_s\, d_j && \text{total energy (minimise)} \end{aligned} $$

\(C\) plays the role of the task success rate, the main metric in zhang2025mtgper and magliarisi2025orbit. \(R\) is the objective of magliarisi2025orbit (Eq. 6). \(\mathcal{E}\) follows the energy-budget model of icdcs_sc.

These three goals conflict. Scheduling more jobs costs more energy, and jobs placed later raise the mean response time. The fitness function combines them into one number with weights. The theory page explains why that is a delicate choice.

A trap that matters for the results

\(R\) is averaged only over the jobs that were scheduled. A schedule that places three short jobs and rejects 97 has an excellent \(R\). The thesis found that long evolutions exploit exactly this. See Experiments & findings.

Why not solve it exactly?

Take the simplest special case: identical satellites, unlimited energy and one common sunset. Asking "can all jobs be finished before sunset?" is then the decision version of \(P\,\|\,C_{\max}\), the classic problem of minimising the makespan on parallel identical machines, which is NP-hard (lenstra1977complexity). Maximising the completion ratio \(C\) answers that question, so our problem is at least as hard, and we should not expect a fast exact algorithm. (Minimising \(R\) alone with every job scheduled would be easy: on identical machines, shortest-job-first is optimal for total completion time. The constraints are what make the problem hard.) Exact ILP formulations exist, but in Magliarisi and Casalicchio's study the ILP had a lower success rate than the heuristics, because it took too long and missed deadlines (icdcs_sc §V-C). A hand-written greedy rule is fast but fixed. GP sits in between: it searches offline for a fast rule that works well on the data.
