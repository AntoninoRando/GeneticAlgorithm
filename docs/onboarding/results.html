Experiments & findings

What has been measured so far, how, and how much weight each number can bear. Sources: thesis/chapters/experiments.tex, thesis/experiments/results/ and the ablation notes in scheduler_gp.h and the readme.

How the experiments are run

  • Train/test split. Worlds 0–19 train, worlds 20–29 test. Each world is cut to its first 100 jobs, and satellites use a fixed per-world seed, so every method sees identical scenarios.
  • Training. The training world changes every 10 generations on a fixed schedule, unlike main.py's convergence-triggered switch, so runs are comparable. Defaults: population 100, depth 4, hoist 0.05, 300 generations, weights (0.60, 0.25, 0.15).
  • Model selection. Fitness is not comparable across worlds, so the 20 fittest individuals are re-scored on all training worlds with the raw objectives. The one with the highest mean \(C\) (ties: lowest \(R\)) is evaluated on the test worlds.
  • Baselines through the same decoder: FCFS (\(-a_j\)), shortest job first (\(-d_j\)), least-loaded (\(-\)completed), most energy left, and the untrained random population.
  • Seeds. 10 per configuration for the main study, 20 + 20 for the ablation.

Code: run_experiments.py, run_ablation.py, make_report.py.

Reading it. The evolved rules cut the mean response time by about 18% against the best baseline, with very little variance across seeds. They do not complete more jobs: \(C\) is no better than random trees and below shortest-job-first. Energy barely differs between methods (about 1%).

Why. \(R\) is averaged only over scheduled jobs, and the fitness is dominated by the normalised cost terms (proof on the theory page). A rule that schedules few, short jobs scores well. Longer training drives the trees toward tiny rules (mean size 2.4 at 1000 generations) that exploit this. The correct conclusion is "the current fitness is misaligned with completing jobs", not "GP cannot beat the baselines on \(C\)".

What the gain really is. The baseline is bimodal: in 16 of 40 runs it ends on a degenerate one-terminal rule such as job.arrivalTime (\(C \approx 0.19\)), while the others reach about 0.24–0.26. With clone retries, 0 of 40 runs fail this way; with three worlds, 1 of 40. Final structural diversity rises from 0.57 to 0.97 and 0.86. So both options mainly prevent premature convergence. The typical successful run improves only a little (median 0.242 → 0.249 / 0.251), and \(R\) gets slightly worse (14.3 → 14.9 / 14.6 min).

Not measured yet: the fixed-bound fitness

The fixed-bound fitness (--fixed-bounds) was added on 2026-11-14 to address the misalignment above. It has no row in this table because it has not been run on the held-out worlds of the original data. On generated worlds it has: there it reaches the completion ceiling, at the cost of a longer response time (Realistic data). That is not a result on the original data. The ablation script has two configurations ready, fixed and fixed+clones+worlds3:

python3 thesis/experiments/run_ablation.py --seeds 30 \
    --configs baseline,fixed,clones+worlds3,fixed+clones+worlds3 \
    --out thesis/experiments/results_fixed

When you add the row, report test \(C\), \(R\) and \(\mathcal{E}\) with the test and an effect size, and remember that the fitness values of the two modes are on different scales and must not be compared with each other.

Not measured yet: the earliest-free tie-break

The decoder option --earliest-free-tie-break (what it does) was added on 2026-11-14. It has no row either. Two things make this experiment different from the others:

  • It changes the decoder, not the search. A rule trained with it must be compared with baselines that were also scored with it. Re-run run_experiments.py's baselines with fe.earliestFreeTieBreak = True before putting numbers side by side.
  • The expected effect has two signs. Response time should drop for job-only rules. Completion ratio may drop, because spreading jobs fragments the batteries (theory). Report both.

What the evolved rules look like

C = 0.248, size 15:
((job.arrivalTime - job.executionTime)
   - ((MAX(job.disk, job.arrivalTime) * MAX(sat.completedTasks, job.executionTime))
      /? MAX(sat.networkBusyUntil, sat.bandwidth)))

C = 0.191, size 1:
job.arrivalTime

The large rule combines a shortest-job-like term (\(-e_j\)) with arrival time, satellite load and bandwidth. Note sat.networkBusyUntil, which is always 0, so MAX(sat.networkBusyUntil, sat.bandwidth) is just sat.bandwidth.

Careful with "FCFS-like"

The thesis calls the one-terminal rule job.arrivalTime "FCFS-like". Because a higher score wins, it actually schedules the latest arrival first (LIFO), the opposite of the FCFS baseline \(-a_j\). Try both in the playground. Reported as a wording issue in Known quirks.

How much to trust these numbers

  • One trace. Train and test worlds come from the same 300-minute simulation, and the test windows are adjacent in time to the training ones.
  • Low completion everywhere (0.2–0.27), mostly because the energy budget fits only 1–2 jobs per satellite (data page). Conclusions may change under other loads.
  • Small differences. Many configurations differ by about one standard deviation over 10 seeds. The main parameter study ran no significance test, and the ablation p-values are not corrected for 10 comparisons (statistics).
  • Model selection uses training \(C\), not the fitness the GP optimised.
