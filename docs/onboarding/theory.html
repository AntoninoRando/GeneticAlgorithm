1. Why a hyper-heuristic

A hyper-heuristic searches a space of heuristics rather than a space of solutions (burke2013hyperheuristics). For scheduling, GP is the dominant way to do this: it evolves priority functions (dispatching rules) that a fixed procedure applies (branke2016automated, nguyen2017gpsurvey §3.2, zhang2021gpbook).

The argument for our setting has two parts:

  • Decision cost. Using an evolved rule costs one tree evaluation per candidate pair. Sun et al. report under 0.02 s per decision (mtgp_multicloud §5.3). Satellite schedulers need exactly this small footprint; that is why heuristics were preferred to ILP and to GA or deep-learning solvers in the first place (icdcs_sc).
  • Generalisation. A rule can be applied to snapshots it has never seen. A directly evolved schedule cannot. That is why we train on some worlds and test on others (results).

GP has also been applied to satellite edge computing specifically, in a dynamic multi-tree form (zhang2025mtgper).

2. How big is the search space?

Let \(T(d)\) be the number of distinct trees of depth at most \(d\), with \(n_t = 18\) terminals, \(n_1 = 3\) unary and \(n_2 = 6\) binary operators. A tree is either a terminal, a unary operator over a smaller tree, or a binary operator over two smaller trees:

$$T(0) = n_t, \qquad T(d) = n_t + n_1\,T(d-1) + n_2\,T(d-1)^2 .$$

The space roughly squares at each extra level. Many trees are equivalent (\(x+y = y+x\)), but even so, exhaustive or random search is hopeless at the depth-4 cap of our experiments. The question is whether GP's selection plus variation does better than random sampling; the results compare against the random initial population for exactly that reason.

3. One tree or several?

Our tree scores (job, satellite) pairs, so one function answers both "which job next?" and "where?". Most recent papers evolve one tree per decision: routing plus queuing (zhang2025mtgper, gp_fog, zhang2018mtgp), or task, cloud and instance (mtgp_multicloud). Nguyen et al. warn that every added rule enlarges the search space (nguyen2017gpsurvey §5.2). Sun et al.'s own ablation found no significant gain from the extra tree in 3 of their 4 small scenarios (mtgp_multicloud Table 7). Starting with one tree is therefore defensible. A multi-tree version is the natural extension, and it is listed in open questions.

4. Tournament selection: exact probabilities

Sort the \(N\) individuals by fitness, rank 1 being the worst and rank \(N\) the best, and assume no ties. A tournament draws \(k\) ranks uniformly with replacement (as the code does) and returns the maximum. The winner has rank \(\le i\) exactly when all \(k\) draws have rank \(\le i\):

$$\Pr[\text{winner} \le i] = \left(\frac{i}{N}\right)^{k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_i = \Pr[\text{winner} = i] = \frac{i^{k} - (i-1)^{k}}{N^{k}} .$$

For the best individual, \(p_N = 1 - (1 - 1/N)^k \approx k/N\). This is the order-statistics analysis of tournament selection in Blickle and Thiele's comparison of selection schemes (blickle1996selection). They also give an approximate takeover time, the number of generations that selection alone needs to fill the population with copies of the best individual:

$$\tau^{*} \approx \frac{\ln N + \ln \ln N}{\ln k} .$$

Selection pressure calculator

Applied to our defaults (\(N = 100\), \(k = 5\), 98 parent draws per generation): the best individual wins a tournament with probability 4.9%, so it is chosen as a parent about 4.8 times per generation. The worst half of the population together wins only \(0.5^5 \approx 3\%\) of tournaments. Selection alone would take over the population in about 4 generations.

That is strong pressure. It helps explain why 16 of 40 baseline runs collapsed to a one-terminal rule, and why the options that restore diversity (clone retries, more worlds) are the ones that helped (results). \(k = 2\), as in zhang2025mtgper, gives \(p_N \approx 2\%\) and a takeover time of about 9 generations.

Why tournaments fit our fitness

\(p_i\) depends only on the rank, so any strictly increasing transformation of the fitness leaves selection unchanged. Our fitness is re-normalised every generation, which changes its scale but not its ranking. Fitness-proportionate selection, by contrast, would react to the arbitrary scale.

5. The fitness: weighted sum and normalisation

What a weighted sum can and cannot find

Maximising \(w_C C - w_R R - w_E \mathcal E\) with positive weights always returns a Pareto-optimal point: no other schedule is at least as good on all three objectives and strictly better on one. But it can only reach points on the convex part of the Pareto front, and evenly spaced weights do not give evenly spaced solutions (das1997weighted). The common alternatives are:

  • Lexicographic: optimise the primary objective, then the secondary within a tolerance. Magliarisi uses this for the hierarchical ILP and warns that arbitrary weights on non-homogeneous metrics (seconds and joules) bias the solution (magliarisi2026thesis §6.3.2).
  • Pareto-based selection such as NSGA-II (deb2002nsga2), which returns a set of trade-off rules instead of one.

The normalisation changes the balance

\(\hat R\) and \(\hat{\mathcal E}\) are min-max normalised within the population, while \(C\) is not. Take two individuals \(a\) and \(b\) where \(a\) has the population's best costs (\(\hat R = \hat{\mathcal E} = 0\)) and \(b\) the worst (\(\hat R = \hat{\mathcal E} = 1\)). Then \(b\) has the higher fitness only if

$$w_C\,(C_b - C_a) > w_R + w_E \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_b - C_a > \frac{0.25 + 0.15}{0.60} \approx 0.67 .$$

In our data every reasonable rule (baselines, random trees, evolved rules) lands between about 0.19 and 0.27 on the test worlds (results), so completion differences between sensible rules are below 0.1. No such advantage can make up for being the costliest. Fitness is effectively decided by response time and energy, and \(C\) acts mainly as a tie-breaker. This is the formal reason behind the misalignment the thesis observed: more generations, lower \(R\), lower \(C\). The weight study supports it, since a completion-only fitness gave the highest \(C\) (results).

Open design question

A fixed-bound normalisation (as in icdcs_sc, dividing energy by the battery size and time by the largest response), a lexicographic fitness with \(C\) first, or a Pareto method would each remove this imbalance. Any of them is a change to the algorithm and needs the full literature-and-experiment treatment from CLAUDE.md.

Population-relative normalisation also makes the fitness non-stationary: the same tree scores differently in different populations. Selection and elitism are unaffected, since they only compare individuals of the same generation, but fitness values cannot be compared across generations or worlds.

6. Bloat, caps and hoist

Bloat is the growth of GP trees without a matching improvement in fitness. Luke and Panait compare the main bloat-control methods, with Koza-style depth limiting as the standard baseline (luke2006bloat). This project uses a depth cap (as in all the scheduling papers), a size cap, and hoist mutation.

The size cap is implied by the depth cap

Every operator has arity at most 2, so level \(\ell\) of a tree holds at most \(2^\ell\) nodes. A tree of depth \(\le D\) therefore has at most

$$\sum_{\ell=0}^{D} 2^{\ell} = 2^{D+1} - 1 \;<\; 2^{D+1}$$

nodes. The check size > 2^(maxDepth+1) in TreeOperators::violatesLimits can never fail on a tree that passes the depth check. It is harmless, but it is not an independent constraint.

Hoist mostly produces a single leaf

Hoist picks a non-root node uniformly. In a full binary tree with \(n\) nodes there are \((n+1)/2\) leaves, so the probability that the new tree is a single terminal is

$$\Pr[\text{leaf}] = \frac{(n+1)/2}{n-1} > \frac12 .$$

Unary operators only shift this a little. Hoist is therefore a very strong shrinking operator: at rate 0.2, about 10% of all offspring become one-node trees every generation. That fits the thesis finding that hoist 0.2 collapses the mean size to 1.2. Hoist itself is a project addition with no source in the scheduling papers. A variant restricted to internal nodes would be gentler.

7. Terminal weighting as maximum likelihood

With --terminal-weighting, mutation stops drawing terminals uniformly and favours the terminals found in the best trees (dtgpam §IV-D, yang2025thesis §3.2.4; the idea goes back to riley2016terminal). Let \(x_i\) be how often terminal \(i\) appears in the best 10% of trees, \(n\) the number of terminals and \(r \in [0,1]\) a sampling ratio. Mutation draws terminal \(i\) with probability

$$P_i = r\,p_i + \frac{1-r}{n}$$

a mix of a learned distribution \(p\) and the uniform one. The \(p\) that makes the observed counts most likely solves the concave problem

$$\max_{p}\; \sum_i x_i \ln\!\Big(r\,p_i + \tfrac{1-r}{n}\Big) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad p_i \ge 0,\ \ \textstyle\sum_i p_i = 1 .$$

Its KKT conditions give, with \(c = \frac{1-r}{nr}\) and a multiplier \(\nu\) fixed by \(\sum_i p_i = 1\):

$$p_i = \max\!\Big(0,\ \frac{x_i}{\nu} - c\Big).$$

This is a water-filling solution. Sort the \(x_i\) in decreasing order, and find the largest \(k\) such that the \(k\) most frequent terminals stay above the water level with \(\nu_k = \frac{x_1 + \dots + x_k}{1 + kc}\). Cost: \(O(n \log n)\). The code is SchedulerGP::solveTerminalWeights.

Worked example (from Yang's thesis, checked by the unit tests): \(x = (3, 5, 8)\), \(r = \tfrac12\), so \(c = \tfrac13\). With all three active, \(\nu = 16 / (1 + 1) = 8\), and every \(x_i/8 > \tfrac13\), so

$$p = \Big(\tfrac38 - \tfrac13,\ \tfrac58 - \tfrac13,\ 1 - \tfrac13\Big) = \Big(\tfrac{1}{24},\ \tfrac{7}{24},\ \tfrac{2}{3}\Big), \qquad P = \Big(\tfrac{3}{16},\ \tfrac{5}{16},\ \tfrac{1}{2}\Big).$$
An insight worth knowing

In the example \(P_i = x_i / \sum x\), exactly the empirical frequencies. That is no accident. When every terminal is active, \(\nu = r\sum_k x_k\), so \(P_i = r\,x_i/\nu = x_i/\sum_k x_k\). The mixture with the uniform distribution only matters when some terminals are rare enough to hit \(p_i = 0\). Then the maximum-likelihood fit keeps a floor of \((1-r)/n\) for them, instead of 0.

The ratio follows the "log rev" schedule \(r(g) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + 2\ln(g+1)}\), which the thesis found best among six schedules (yang2025thesis Table 3.9). It starts at 0 (uniform) and is capped at 0.9 here, a value it reaches at \(g \approx 89\). Without the cap, a terminal missing from the elite could never be drawn again in our long runs.

8. No free lunch, baselines and statistics

The no-free-lunch theorems say that, averaged over all possible problems, no search algorithm beats any other (wolpert1997nfl). So "GP works" is never a general claim: it must be shown on this problem, against simple alternatives run through the same decoder. That is why the experiments include FCFS, shortest-job-first, least-loaded, most-energy and the untrained random population.

Because GP is stochastic, one run proves nothing. The project's rules for experimental claims:

  • Several independent seeded runs per configuration: 10–20 here, 30–50 in the papers (mtgp_multicloud §5.2, dtgpam §VI).
  • A rank-based test such as the Mann–Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test (mann1947test), which assumes no particular distribution. Test-set completion ratios are bounded and often bimodal, so a t-test would be inappropriate.
  • Correct for multiple comparisons. The ablation compares 10 options with the baseline at \(\alpha = 0.05\). With a Bonferroni correction the threshold becomes \(0.05/10 = 0.005\): cloneRetries (\(p = 0.005\)) is borderline and three worlds (\(p = 0.014\)) does not pass. The confirmation run on fresh seeds is what really supports them.
  • Report effect sizes and medians, not only p-values: the gains were mostly in avoiding failed runs, not in better typical runs.
