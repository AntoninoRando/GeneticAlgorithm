2. What happens to one task

  1. The task is created. It gets an arrival minute \(a_j\), an execution time \(e_j\) (5 to 30 minutes of computing) and a transfer time \(t_j\) (1 to 8 minutes to move its data). Its duration is \(d_j = e_j + t_j\).
  2. It flies up to the access point and waits there.
  3. Feasibility. For every satellite \(s\), two hard rules are checked. \(f_s\) is the minute at which the satellite is free, \(\omega_s\) its sunset, \(E_s\) its energy left, and \(\rho_s = 1\) its energy use per minute: $$f_s + d_j \le \omega_s \quad\text{(finish before sunset)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \rho_s\, d_j \le E_s \quad\text{(enough energy)}$$ A satellite that fails one of them shows "✕" and is out, whatever its score would be.
  4. Scoring. The formula in the box is evaluated for every pair that is left. This formula is the thing genetic programming evolves; here you choose it by hand. The pair with the highest score wins: $$(j^*, s^*) = \arg\max_{\text{feasible } (j,\,s)} \ \text{formula}(j, s)$$ With several tasks waiting, the formula chooses the task and the satellite at once.
  5. Commit. The task starts when the satellite is free and ends \(d_j\) minutes later. The satellite's state changes: \(f_{s^*} \leftarrow f_{s^*} + d_{j^*}\) and \(E_{s^*} \leftarrow E_{s^*} - \rho_{s^*} d_{j^*}\). Its battery bar shrinks.
  6. The task hops from the access point to the chosen satellite and joins its queue.
  7. If no pair is feasible, the decoder stops. Every task still waiting is rejected: it turns red and falls back to the ground. A rejected task lowers the completion ratio \(C\).

Steps 3 to 5 and step 7 are one round of the greedy decoder, detail::greedyDecode in fitness_evaluator.h. The decoder playground shows the same rounds as a table, on a world small enough to check by hand.

The panel "This world so far" shows the three objectives of the problem page: the completion ratio \(C\) (scheduled tasks ÷ tasks decided so far, that is scheduled plus rejected), the mean response time \(R\) (end minute − arrival minute, over scheduled tasks) and the energy spent \(\mathcal{E}\). It does not show a fitness, because the fitness compares one formula with the rest of a population, and here there is only one formula.

4. Things to try

Five small experiments
  • Follow one task. Press Create task once and read the log line: which satellite won, with which score, and how many hops the task flew.
  • Empty the batteries. Keep pressing Create 5. The bars shrink, satellites turn grey, and at some point tasks come back red. Then choose "Like our data" under Batteries and sunsets: with the energy of the real worlds a satellite can pay for one or two tasks only. This is why the completion ratio is low in our experiments (energy is the binding constraint).
  • See the pile-up. Go back to "Roomy", choose Shortest job first and create ten tasks, one at a time. They queue on S0 until it cannot take another one, then on S1, while thirteen satellites stay idle. The formula reads the task only, so every satellite gets the same score, and a tie goes to the lowest satellite number. Now tick Earliest-free tie-break, press New world and repeat: each task goes to a satellite that is free, and the mean response time (R) drops (ties).
  • See the batch. Untick Schedule tasks as they arrive, press Create 5, wait until five tasks hang under the access point, then press One round five times. With Shortest job first the shortest task leaves first, not the oldest one. The formula picks the task too.
  • Write a rule. Type sat.remainingEnergy - 3 * sat.completedTasks and press Enter. Does it spread the tasks better than Most energy left? Compare \(C\) and \(R\) after 40 tasks. Reload the page before each rule: both then start from world 1 and get the same 40 tasks.

5. What is real and what is simplified

The picture adds things that the program does not have, and leaves things out. Know which is which before you explain the project with it.

In this viewIn the real program
Same: the two feasibility rules, "highest score wins", both tie-breaks, the commit, and "stop when nothing is feasible".The script replays the worked examples of the decoder page and gets the same schedules (section 6).
Tasks arrive one by one, and the decoder runs a round whenever a task is waiting.The decoder gets a snapshot: about 100 tasks, all waiting at the same minute \(\tau\), decoded in one go. Untick Schedule tasks as they arrive to get closer to that.

The flight of a task takes about a second per hop.There is no flight. The transfer time \(t_j\) is part of the duration \(d_j\), and it is the same on every satellite.

The numbers on this page mean nothing

The worlds are made up, like those of demo mode. Use the view to understand and to explain, never to compare rules or to choose a parameter. Real comparisons need the held-out worlds, several seeds and a statistical test (experiments).

6. Where the code is

  • docs/onboarding/assets/viz3d.js. Part 1, MODEL, mirrors the selection and commit blocks of detail::greedyDecode. Part 2, SCENE, only draws.
  • docs/onboarding/assets/formula.js: the formula parser, shared with the decoder playground.
  • Self-test. Open the browser console (F12) on this page and run Viz3D.selfTest(). It decodes the worked example and the tie-break examples of the decoder page and must answer ok: true. Run it after any change to part 1.
  • If you change the decoder in C++, change part 1 of viz3d.js, the self-test and this page in the same commit (where do I start).
